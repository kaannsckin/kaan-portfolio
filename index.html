Stop 02

From playing 100 levels to designing my own game

Game design case · University years

My interest in the game industry pulled me into several case studies during university. One of them was about tap-to-blast design and game analysis, prepared for a mobile games studio.

I played Royal Match and Toon Blast up to level 50 each and took notes on everything: mechanics, level design, difficulty curves, power-ups and their combinations, obstacles, UI, animation and social features. I also asked players who had reached advanced levels what kept them coming back.

Royal Match

  • Swipe to match, with a castle story that gives stars a purpose.
  • New obstacles arrive gradually: boxes at level 6, cupboards at 11, potion bottles at 41.
  • Difficulty ramps around level 20. Bonus goals and stars invite replay.
  • On failure, the game hides the remaining goals before offering extra moves. That makes players hesitate before spending.

Toon Blast

  • Tap groups of two or more. Very accessible and fast.
  • Rescue missions and decoration mode add side loops.
  • Simple layouts and rare difficulty spikes. Good for casual play, repetitive for experienced players.
  • No star rating or secondary goals, so there is little reason to replay a level.

The gap I saw: tap-to-blast is easy to learn, but the tap itself carries almost no strategy. That became the design question.

My game concept

Solve Stop

A hitchhiker travels across very different regions. To move on, he solves puzzles that build the tools and vehicles he needs for the next route. Rubis, a cheerful parrot, guides him and the player.

The twist: matched items do not disappear. They upgrade. Screws become gears, gears become motors, motors become vehicles. The upgraded item appears in the cell you tapped, so where you tap matters.

ToolsVehicles. Each one can travel further than the last, so a route's goal is a set of vehicles.

Tap a group of three or more identical items. The upgrade lands where you tapped. Cleared cells refill in place instead of falling from above, exactly as in the design document.

Stop 03

STARS, a student network for a bank

TEB İcat Çıkar competition · 3rd place

Most company programs only accept third and fourth-year students. I designed STARS to reach students earlier, in prep, first and second year, when a bank can still become part of their story.

STARs are selected students who represent TEB on campus, organise events and act as the bridge between students and the bank. In return they get support for their studies and direct access to people inside TEB.

  • CompetitionsTeams of STARs receive a budget and try to grow it legally, mentored by TEB interns. The same format later runs on campuses with STARs as mentors.
  • EducationBook and study support, plus a summer school in software, project management, data analysis, economics and accounting.
  • Campus eventsSponsorship for student clubs' concerts, theatre, case competitions and summits.
  • CampsShort trips for top STARs from different cities, with senior TEB managers joining.
  • VisibilitySTARs appear in TEB's own advertising, so students outside the program see how much the bank values it.
  • Reverse mentoringMentoring goes both ways. STARs also work on real problems inside the bank and bring a younger view.
Stop 04

Two Starbucks problems, one before the counter and one behind it

Stars for Starbucks program · Summer 2024

Project 1 · Filter coffee

Brew what will be drunk within the hour

With the new refill campaign, filter coffee demand changed. Brewed coffee lives for one hour, and each machine has two urns that brew quarter, half or full batches. Brew too little and guests wait. Brew too much and it goes down the drain.

  1. Forecast. SARIMA demand per store in half-hour slots, by Tall, Grande and Venti, with adjustments for special days.
  2. Analyse. A probabilistic inventory model converts demand into litres, a reorder point and safety stock.
  3. Model. A desktop tool: pick country, region and store, then see the forecast, an hourly dashboard and graphs.

The dashboard tells the barista which machine mode to run for the past, current and next hour. The same model could extend to food and to the "Live Order" system in development.

Project 2 · Queue experience

Make the wait worth something

At peak hours, guests wait. Queue optimisation can shorten the wait, but it cannot remove it. So the second idea changed what the wait feels like.

Stars Game of the Day is a short daily game inside the mobile app. After ordering, guests can play while their drink is prepared and earn Stars. It rewards mobile ordering, which also takes pressure off the counter.

Product line · Tapan and Lee

Tapan, a smart farming network

Farmers deal with missing know-how, middlemen who set low prices, unpredictable weather and a lonely profession. Tapan puts a social network, farm news, an AI farm assistant and a direct marketplace in one app.

Social

Posts, follows and a discover feed by interest and location. Farmers share problems, wins and practices such as heirloom seeds.

News

Local farm news, ministry announcements, grants and global market updates, filtered by location and crop.

My Farm

A project per field and a digital ID per animal: irrigation, fertiliser and spraying on a calendar, vaccine schedules and milk yield.

Market

Direct listings with photos and video, AI price guidance, ratings, and a "smart farming product" badge for produce tracked in My Farm.

  4. StrategyYears of data suggest the most profitable crop for next season.

Stop 07

What should a tutor be paid next hour?

Graduation project with Kunduz · Grade AA · with İbrahim Kantarcı and Zeynep Betül Özarslandan · advisor Aybek Korugan

Kunduz students photograph a question and get a solution. If the explanation is not enough, they tap "Contact tutor" and a follow-up chat opens. Demand for those follow-ups swings hour by hour, and tutor campaigns were priced by hand.

20M+records analysed
0.85R² on the test set
0.912price elasticity of tutor supply
~95%fulfilment at the suggested price

  1. The process

    A follow-up starts when the student is not satisfied. Kunduz creates it, a tutor picks it up and chats. If nobody closes the session, the system finalises it after 24 hours. Tutors can start, pause and resume active sessions.

    The trade-off was clear: a higher fulfilment rate costs more. The goal was to decide how much tutor supply each hour needs, and at what price.

  2. The data

    We focused on high school maths, the course with the most demand and the most anomalies. Hourly demand was classified into five density classes. The summer drop and the exam season peaks are clear.

  3. Forecast the next hour

    An XGBoost model uses the previous hour, the same hour yesterday and last week, rolling extremes, and calendar signals. The previous hour carries more than half of the importance.

    R²MAERMSE
    Train0.911810.5416.13
    Test0.847511.0516.99

  4. Three strategies, one choice

    Dynamic categories and scheduling. Needed category thresholds the company had not set, and more data than we had.

    Dynamic bonus. Bonuses when load crosses a threshold. Tutors were not sensitive enough to bonuses, and their response was hard to predict.

    Dynamic pricing. One adaptive hourly price that merges base price and bonus, balancing cost and fulfilment, and fits the current system.

  5. Assumptions

    M/M/c/c queue. We could not extract a reneging rate from history, so M/M/c was out. With M/M/c/c, fulfilment is one minus the Erlang B blocking probability.

    Stable service time. Service rate shifted once, in April 2024, then stayed steady.

    Price moves supply. Next hour's tutors = Ct · exp(e · (Pt+1 − Pt) / Pt).

What I would tell the next team

The model works best in busy periods and in hourly steps. It uses a point forecast, so uncertainty in demand is not priced in yet. And the queue model tends to under-estimate the real fulfilment rate, which makes its prices conservative rather than risky.
